## Connacht arms — the blazon (verbatim)

> "The technical heraldic description (blazon) of the Connacht arms is: Party Per Pale Argent and Azure, in the first an eagle dimidiated and displayed Sable, in the second issuant from the partition an arm embowed and vested, the hand holding a sword erect, all Argent. This peculiar design is unique in Irish provincial heraldry. Unlike the three antique crowns of Munster (representing the three Desmond/Ormond/Thomond lordships) or the Harp of Leinster (sovereignty of Ireland), the Connacht arms feature a violent fusion: an eagle split in half vertically, joined directly to a human arm wielding a sword. In heraldry, this technique is known as dimidiation. Historically, dimidiation was used to join the arms of a husband and wife (Baron and Femme) or a King and a Town. It implies a marriage, a union of two distinct bodies into one legal entity. The fact that the eagle is cut in half suggests that the 'Imperial' power is incomplete without the 'Martial' arm, and vice versa." (p. 2)

However, deep research confirms a far more significant origin: the Schottenklöster (Scottish/Irish Monasteries) of Regensburg, Bavaria. The Irish Benedictine monks of St. James (St. Jakob) in Regensburg, founded in the 11th century by Marianus Scottus, utilized a coat of arms combining the Imperial Eagle (Reichsadler) of the Holy Roman Empire with an Arm holding a Sword. The Imperial Protectorate: The monastery was under the direct protection of the Holy Roman Emperor (Henry IV and his successors). By adopting the Eagle, the Irish monks signaled that they were not merely local clerics but agents of the Universal Church and the Universal Empire. The Gaelic Patronage: The monastery was funded by the Kings of Munster (MacCarthy, O'Brien) and Connacht (O'Connor). King Ruaidrí Ua Conchobair (Rory O'Connor), the last High King of Ireland before the Norman invasion, was a primary benefactor. It is highly probable that the monks 'granted' these arms to the O'Connors as a sign of this trans-European alliance, effectively recognizing the O'Connors as 'Imperial Princes' within the framework of Christendom." (p. 2)
