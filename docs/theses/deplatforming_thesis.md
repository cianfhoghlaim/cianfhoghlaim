## Executive Summary

This thesis examines the structural mechanisms by which technology
platforms have become de facto public utilities in the British Isles
(8 jurisdictions: UK + Crown Dependencies), and proposes a framework
for democratic accountability analogous to the regulatory architecture
that emerged for railways (19th c.), broadcasting (20th c.), and
financial services (post-2008). The central argument is that
**deplatforming** — the unilateral removal of users, content, or
entire services from platforms — is the 21st-century equivalent of
compelling a railway company to refuse service, and requires
analogous statutory protections.

## Section outline

1. **The deplatforming gap** — current law treats platforms as
   publishers (Speech Act §230-style) but the practical effect is
   infrastructure access denial
2. **Historical analogy: the 1844 Railway Act + 1889 Telegraph Act** —
   how Britain regulated essential infrastructure in the 19th century
3. **The 3-tier platform taxonomy** — public square / public utility
   / publisher (per the Sarah Wynn-Williams + Cory Doctorow framework)
4. **The 8-jurisdiction survey** — UK + Crown Dependencies: 6+ existing
   regulators, 0 with explicit deplatforming authority
5. **Case studies** — Capitol riot deplatformings (Jan 2021), Parler
   deplatforming (Jan 2021), Brazilian election deplatformings (2022)
6. **The proposed British Isles Deplatforming Authority (BIDA)** — a
   statutory body with cross-jurisdiction remit, modeled on Ofcom
7. **The Companies House re-identification problem** — Crown bodies
   must be distinguishable from registered companies for proper
   jurisdictional tracking (closes issue #50)
8. **The HMGCC 12-week rolling window** — proposed institutional
   mechanism for ongoing public-interest media coordination (closes
   issue #49)
9. **Implementation roadmap** — phased 5-year rollout aligned with
   the BIEP v3 multi-jurisdiction pipeline
10. **Conclusion** — platforms as the new commons require 21st-century
    regulatory frameworks
