# Aos Sí

In [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_folklore) and [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_folklore) folklore, the **Aos Sí** ([Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language): _[aes sídhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aes_s%C3%ADdhe)_) are the supernatural race comparable to the [fairies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairy) or [elves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elf) of other traditions. The term is the Irish for "folk of the _[sídhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%ADdhe)_" — the burial mounds or ancient tumuli of Ireland. In modern Irish, the Aos Sí are often simply called _na sídhe_ ("the sídhe folk") or, in English, **the fairies**.

## Etymology

The word **sídhe** (singular) is the modern Irish reflex of Old Irish _síde_, which is in turn the dative singular form of _síd_, meaning "a [fairy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairy) mound or hill". The word _síd_ derives from [proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic_language) **\*sīdos**, meaning "abode" or "seat", which is also the source of the Latin word _sēdēs_ ("seat, residence") and the Greek word _hedos_ ("seat, foundation"). The etymological chain suggests that the _sídhe_ were originally understood as the divine inhabitants of the burial mounds, with the mounds themselves representing the thrones or seats of the otherworld.

## Origin in the Tuatha Dé Danann

According to the medieval Irish tradition (as preserved in the _Lebor Gabála Érenn_), the **Tuatha Dé Danann** were the fifth and last of the mythological races to invade Ireland, after the Cessair, the Partholón, the Nemedians, and the Fomorians. After their defeat by the **Milesians** (the ancestors of the modern Irish), the Tuatha Dé Danann withdrew into the _sídhe_ — the burial mounds of Ireland — and became the Aos Sí.

This narrative preserves a fundamental transition in early Irish cosmology: the pre-Christian Irish gods (the Tuatha Dé Danann) were not destroyed but "concealed" within the landscape, and the smaller mounds, ringforts, and cairns of medieval Ireland were understood to be their dwelling-places. This transition is sometimes referred to as the **euhemerization** of the Tuatha Dé Danann — the demotion of high gods to local spirits — but in fact the tradition is more subtle: the Tuatha Dé Danann retained their divine status as the "people of the goddess Danu" while simultaneously being reimagined as the diminutive Aos Sí of medieval folklore.

## Character and behaviour

The Aos Sí are not uniformly benevolent or malevolent; their behaviour toward mortals depends on how mortals treat them. The key principles of the Aos Sí tradition are:

- They dwell within the _sídhe_ (burial mounds, ringforts, certain natural features)
- They can move between their world and the mortal world at will, particularly at **Samhain** (1 November) and **Beltane** (1 May)
- They are diminished in size compared to mortals (in some traditions) or of human size (in others)
- They require a share of the harvest, the milk, and the first fruits of the field
- They are repelled by **iron**, by **salt**, and by the recitation of certain Christian prayers
- They reward mortals who treat them well (with good fortune, with skill in crafts, with fertility of land and livestock) and punish mortals who offend them (with illness, with crop failure, with the kidnapping of children or women)
- They sometimes abduct mortals and bring them to their _sídhe_ for a period that passes as one night in the mortal world but as years or centuries in the otherworld

## Connection to the Cianfhoghlaim claim

The Aos Sí connection is direct: Cian, the Tuatha Dé Danann figure whose name means "enduring one", is the father of Lugh, who led the Tuatha Dé Danann to victory over the Fomorians at the **Second Battle of Mag Tuired**. After the Milesian conquest, Cian (along with all the Tuatha Dé Danann) was reimagined as an Aos Sí figure who would dwell in the _sídhe_ of Ireland. In the Cianfhoghlaim reading, the _sídhe_ are reinterpreted as the **burial mounds and ringforts of Connacht** (particularly the [Grianan of Aileach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grianan_of_Aileach) in Co. Donegal, the seat of the [Northern Uí Néill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_U%C3%AD_N%C3%A9ill) and thus the Cian lineage through Lugaid mac Lóegairi), which are the geographical foundations of the claim of Rí Chonnachta and Rí na hÉireann.
